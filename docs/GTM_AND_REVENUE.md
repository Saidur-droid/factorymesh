# GTM and Revenue

## Commercial thesis

FactoryMesh does not charge because it introduces a buyer to a factory.

FactoryMesh earns its fee when it reduces manufacturing uncertainty by delivering:

**speed + certainty + lower sourcing workload + lower execution risk.**

The commercial promise is:

> **For your exact order, show which factory is truly available now, how much capacity is available, how fast it can produce, the expected cost and execution risk — then let you reserve that capacity immediately.**

## First customer acquisition

### Buyer side
Prioritize buyers with urgent sourcing friction:
- brands entering Bangladesh,
- brands needing overflow capacity,
- buying houses with fragmented supplier coordination,
- brands with seasonal production spikes,
- mid-market international brands without a large Bangladesh sourcing organization.

Founder-led sales initially.

Pitch outcome, not software:

> **“Send us one real production brief. We will return a verified, bookable capacity plan — not a list of factories.”**

### Factory side
Factories join because FactoryMesh can bring qualified demand and improve utilization.

Factory pitch:

> **“Show us where you have real production capacity. We bring demand that fits it.”**

Factory listing/onboarding is free initially. Do not create supply-side friction before buyer demand exists.

## Why buyers should pay

A buyer should pay only when FactoryMesh creates measurable execution value.

The 2.5% launch fee is justified when FactoryMesh can do several of the following:

- reduce a multi-day sourcing process to a credible capacity plan within 24–48 hours,
- replace outreach to 10–20 possible factories with 1–3 executable options,
- verify whether a slot is actually available,
- rank options by cost, deadline, quality, compliance and execution risk,
- recommend one factory or a safer split route,
- turn a recommendation into a capacity reservation,
- give the buyer one production/QC/shipment view,
- surface a backup route when a primary route becomes risky,
- preserve outcome history so the next decision is better.

The buyer is not buying “access to FactoryMesh.” The buyer is buying **production certainty and execution leverage**.

## Launch pricing — locked validation cohort

For the first five paid shipped orders:

### Standard orchestration fee
**2.5% of fulfilled/shipped FOB manufacturing GMV**

Commercial rules:
- no signup fee,
- no mandatory monthly buyer subscription,
- factory listing/onboarding free initially,
- minimum fee: **$1,000 per successful order**,
- standard rate: **2.5%**,
- orders at or above **$250k** may use **2.0%** where scope and unit economics justify it,
- very large programs may use negotiated volume pricing,
- **no successful order = no standard orchestration fee**.

Do not reduce the standard rate merely to win a logo. Validate whether the buyer pays because the operational value is real.

After the first five paid shipped orders, review:
- buyer price objections,
- cost-to-serve,
- operator hours,
- gross margin,
- average GMV,
- repeat rate,
- and whether FactoryMesh replaced meaningful sourcing work.

Only then revise the pricing policy.

## Revenue examples

These are operating examples, not forecasts.

At the standard 2.5% rate:

| Fulfilled order GMV | FactoryMesh revenue |
|---:|---:|
| $50,000 | $1,250 |
| $100,000 | $2,500 |
| $200,000 | $5,000 |

A useful early operating target:

> **10 repeat buyers × $100k fulfilled GMV/month × 2.5% = $25,000 monthly revenue**

The purpose of this model is not to promise revenue. It shows why a small number of high-value repeat buyers can support meaningful B2B economics.

## Phase 2: recurring software
Only after the core transaction loop proves repeat value:
- buyer planning subscription,
- multi-user / enterprise access,
- forward capacity planning,
- scenario routing,
- supplier-performance intelligence,
- API access.

## Phase 3: ecosystem revenue
Only where partner/legal structures are appropriate:
- QC/inspection margin or referral,
- logistics integrations,
- insurance referral/API economics,
- financing referral,
- material sourcing/procurement economics.

FactoryMesh should remain asset-light and should not become the production lender or inventory owner merely to inflate revenue.

## Unit economics discipline

Track per order:
- GMV,
- gross revenue,
- take rate,
- operator hours,
- verification cost,
- inspection/support cost,
- acquisition cost,
- gross margin,
- contribution margin.

Track per buyer:
- acquisition cost,
- first-order payback,
- repeat rate,
- annual fulfilled GMV,
- gross profit,
- time saved / sourcing workload displaced where measurable.

If orchestration revenue is consumed by manual operations, automate or narrow the service before scaling.

## Distribution loops

### Buyer → factory loop
More qualified buyer demand attracts more factories.

### Factory → buyer loop
More verified live capacity gives buyers better availability and faster responses.

### Outcome loop
More completed orders improve capacity confidence and routing quality, which should improve conversion and retention.

## Pricing principle

FactoryMesh should make money when it creates measurable production value:

**faster capacity access + verified availability + better routing + lower coordination burden + better execution visibility.**

Avoid low-value per-seat SaaS as the only business model.

## Commercial anti-pattern

Do not pitch:

> “We introduce you to factories and take 2.5%.”

Pitch:

> **“We convert your production brief into verified, bookable capacity and stay in the workflow through execution. You pay when the order succeeds.”**
